# Research and chart-selection decisions

## What does Jack mean?

A predefined analytic proxy: **male, third class, known age 18–25 inclusive**. No record is literally fictional Jack Dawson. The full 891-row file remains unchanged; age-specific subsets exclude only rows with unrecorded ages.

## Why the three poster charts?

1. **Young women fared better.** Within the same third class and 18–25 age band, women had 14/30 (46.7%) survival versus 10/86 (11.6%) for men. Unlike an all-passenger sex comparison, it directly contextualizes Jack's demographic while keeping class and age range fixed.
2. **Class was not a ladder.** Men aged 18–30: first class 9/22 (40.9%), second class 1/41 (2.4%), third class 19/130 (14.6%). This retains the unusual, inconvenient second-class value instead of cherry-picking first vs third. The first class is a small group. The ordering is also sensitive to group definition and sampling.
3. **No clear family advantage.** In the registered H3 group (third-class men 18–50), recorded close relatives were associated with 3/33 (9.1%) survival versus 25/168 (14.9%) without recorded relatives. This rejects the registered directional hypothesis in the sample, but small samples and the narrow recorded-relative definition limit interpretation.

All charts use survival rates, state numerators/denominators, and display an axis starting at zero.

## What we cut from the poster

- Whole-sample passenger-class rates: seem to show a simple first > second > third pattern, **but this is contradicted by the second/third ordering within young men**. Included in the notebook expressly to demonstrate aggregation can conceal subgroup differences.
- Overall sex/class chart: helpful background, but redundant with the closer-to-Jack first chart. Preserved in the notebook.
- Age-band trends: useful context but confounded by missing ages and not a new part of the poster's core story.
- Missing-age and cabin charts: essential evidence in the notebook but they explain quality, not the main comparative result.
- Embarkation rates and fares: potentially confounded, not sufficiently central to this precise assignment.

## Three data weaknesses

1. **Age missingness:** 177/891 (19.9%), uneven across class and sex. We never invent ages.
2. **Cabin missingness:** 687/891 (77.1%); 479/491 third-class records (97.6%) have no recorded cabin versus only 40/216 (18.5%) first-class records. Cabin availability cannot be treated as comparable across classes.
3. **Small subgroup denominators:** first-class young men n=22, second-class n=41, recorded-family third-class men n=33, with broad Wilson intervals. Avoid strong population-wide or causal conclusions.

The observation that higher class correlates with survival among some groups does not prove changing Jack's ticket would alter his fate. Boarding location, evacuation access, family/social ties, timing and crew decisions are not observed in this table.
