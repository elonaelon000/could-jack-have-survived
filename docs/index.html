FICTIONAL

In James Cameron's Titanic (1997), Jack Dawson is a young artist without money or status. He wins a third-class ticket and meets Rose, a first-class passenger.

Their love story crosses the social divisions of the ship. After the sinking, Jack helps Rose stay on floating debris while he remains in the freezing water. He dies before rescue; she survives.

Why is that ending so painful? Our reading of the story: the audience is invited to imagine their future together, then watches that future disappear. His death makes an enormous disaster feel personal.

Film context: Paramount Pictures. Emotional interpretation is ours, not an audience survey.

THE DETAIL THAT BREAKS THE EASY STORY

Second class came last.

For men aged 18–30 with known ages, second class had 1 survivor out of 41, below third class's 19 out of 130. The overall class ranking does not hold inside this subgroup.

Does this prove second class was more dangerous?

No. This is a descriptive ranking reversal, not proof of a cause or a formal Simpson's paradox. Group composition, missing ages, and small denominators matter. We keep the inconvenient result rather than hide it.

OBSERVED IN THIS SAMPLE

APPROX. 95% WILSON INTERVAL

A group rate is not Jack's destiny. Changing filters selects different people; it does not reveal what would happen if Jack changed his ticket. The interval does not correct selection bias, missing ages, or unmeasured differences.

The twist: even the family result changes with the age window.

In the registered H3 group (third-class men 18–50), recorded relatives correspond to 3/33 survivors versus 25/168 with none recorded. Narrowing to 18–25 gives 3/11 versus 7/75. That is an exploratory reversal, not permission to rewrite the original hypothesis.

Try the family filter in both age ranges. Also remember: SibSp + Parch records certain relatives, not friends, romantic partners, or everybody travelling together.

We preserve the operational definitions from the first research commit, before outcome-based analysis. Here, survived means the observed direction matches the hypothesis—not statistical significance.

Why do the age windows differ?

They were specified separately: Jack's proxy is 18–25; H1 uses adults 18–50; H2 uses men 18–30; H3 uses third-class men 18–50. We show the windows openly rather than pretending the results describe one identical group. Changing a window now is an exploratory sensitivity check, not preregistration.

05 INTERROGATE THE DATA THREE PROBLEMS / CODE-BACKED EVIDENCE

The records have
blind spots.

01 / MISSING AGES
177/891

Nearly one age in five is missing.

Missingness differs across recorded groups. We keep the raw data and exclude unknown ages only from age-dependent comparisons. We do not invent membership in the Jack-like group.

02 / MISSING CABINS
687/891

A cabin map would be misleading.

Cabin is missing for 97.6% of third-class records but 18.5% of first-class records. It cannot provide a comparable evacuation-location measure across classes.

03 / TINY COMPARISONS
10people

One outcome moves the rate by ten points.

Only ten first-class men aged 18–25 are recorded, and three survived. An impressive-looking percentage can rest on very few people. We show denominators and uncertainty.

ONE LIMITATION THAT MATTERS

No comparison in this dataset can establish whether changing Jack's ticket would have saved him: lifeboat access and evacuation timing are unobserved. The 891 training records are also not the full passenger-and-crew population.
